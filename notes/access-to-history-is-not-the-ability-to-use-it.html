Access to History Is Not the Ability to Use It

Access to history is not the ability to use it

A robot policy that sees only the current frame knows where things are, not where they are going. Motion, contact, and how far a task has already run live in the last few seconds of video, so feeding the policy more history looks like the obvious way to make it better. Context length is easy to raise and easy to measure.

A paper posted to arXiv on 7 October 2026 tested that assumption and found the history only helps if the model was trained to predict from it. The work is Long-WAM, a framework for scaling the visual context of a world-action model, the class of model that predicts future video and turns the prediction into actions.

One knob, three benchmarks

The authors train a family of models and then vary a single quantity, how many seconds of real observation the policy conditions on before it acts. Everything else about the decision stays fixed, so the comparison isolates the value of past evidence instead of changing how far ahead the model predicts or how long an action chunk it commits to. Zero history keeps only the current frame, and missing early frames repeat the first one.

On the RoboCasa GR-1 tabletop tasks, where each episode chains several object transfers, the same recipe trained with 2.4 seconds of history scores 63.3 percent. Trained with 19.2 seconds it scores 78.7 percent. A second benchmark, LIBERO-Long, needs far less: 2.4 seconds of history lifts success from 94.5 percent to 99.5 percent.

The peak is not the interesting part. Who reaches it is.

The pretraining decides

Three video foundations are compared under the same causal adaptation. Wan2.2 was pretrained bidirectionally, LongLive-2.0 autoregressively, and LongLive2.0-Robot autoregressively on robot and egocentric video. Autoregressive pretraining learns to predict each chunk from the chunks before it, which is the same history-to-future ordering a controller needs at run time.

Only the autoregressive foundations gain from a longer window. The bidirectional variant climbs from 61.7 percent at 2.4 seconds to 64.1 percent at 9.6 seconds, then falls back to 61.6 percent at 19.2 seconds, a net wash. The two autoregressive variants add 12.4 and 11.0 points across the same interval. The robot-domain model's lead over the bidirectional one grows from 3.3 points without history to 17.1 points at 19.2 seconds. Every variant is adapted the same way and every variant can read the history, and only the ones pretrained to predict from it turn the history into success.

The paper's own phrasing is that access to history is not the same as using it.

Context has a price and a ceiling

History is not free at inference. The RTX 5090 pipeline runs 107.4 milliseconds per action chunk, and at 19.2 seconds of history that rises to 341.0 milliseconds, a 3.2 times increase for eight times the window. The 107.4 figure already includes a 3.3 times speedup over a BF16 eager run, built from NVFP4 quantization, streaming VAE encoding that prepares observations while the robot is still moving, and asynchronous execution that overlaps inference with the chunk being executed. The authors get this without the blending or prefix-guidance tricks other systems use to keep consecutive chunks consistent.

The window also stops helping at some point. At 38.4 seconds, three times the average training trajectory of 12.1 seconds, success falls to 75.2 and 74.2 percent. The authors note that 80.4 percent of the sampled history frames at that length are padding, and read the decline as thin data rather than a hard memory limit.

What moved with it

Two other choices paid off in the same study. Predicting the near future before denoising the action, which the authors call inverse dynamics modeling, beat both action-only denoising and joint video-action denoising on LIBERO-Long, 99.5 against 94.5 and 97.8 percent. The future video is never decoded to pixels either. Only its latent cache is kept, which is what makes the prediction affordable.

As an executor under a high-level planner the policy also scaled. On RoboCasa365, pairing the unchanged checkpoint with GPT-6 Astra raised overall success from 31.4 to 54.4 percent, against 25.2 percent for the planner alone. The planner added 23.0 points for this policy and 13.6 for a weaker baseline, which the authors read as stronger execution letting planning pay off more. On real hardware, a Unitree G1 stacks cups on a conveyor moving at 7.5 centimeters per second in 95 percent of trials, where two named baselines succeeded in none of twenty.

What to take from it

What decides the gain is the training objective, not the length of the window. A policy can hold twenty seconds of video and still ignore it, and the study shows that a longer window on the wrong pretraining buys nothing and then starts to cost.

For anyone building a video-conditioned controller, the useful question is not how much history to feed the model. It is whether the pretraining objective and the run-time context share the same ordering. When they do not, the extra frames are latency with no return.
