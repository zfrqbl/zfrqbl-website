A Stalking Horse Bid Is Not a Rescue

The bid is a floor, not a rescue

Character.AI filed a voluntary Chapter 11 case in the U.S. Bankruptcy Court for the District of Delaware on October 12, 2026, to pursue a sale of all or substantially all of its assets. The company's own filing puts assets and liabilities between $50 million and $100 million, which is a thin estate for a platform that spent years as one of the largest AI companion apps. Its announcement also says what bankruptcy statements usually leave out: if no bid arrives, the platform will be wound down.

There is exactly one bid, and it is not for the company. Disney Worldwide Services is the stalking horse bidder for a non-exclusive perpetual license of certain Character.AI intellectual property and certain company claims, at $20 million, plus transition services delivered by former Character.AI employees Disney hired last month. A stalking horse sets a floor. The transaction is subject to higher or better offers, and the company has asked the court to approve bidding procedures to govern that auction.

What the twenty million buys

A license. Not the platform, not the user base, and not the obligations attached to either. The IP and certain claims go to Disney under a non-exclusive perpetual license. Character.AI keeps operating through the process, which the company expects to close in late November or early December 2026.

The people moved first. Disney hired former Character.AI staff last month, alongside the company's former CEO Karandeep Singh Anand, and those are the employees delivering the transition services in the $20 million agreement. TechCrunch reports there is not yet a bid for the company's assets or a buyer.

The technology already left

This is the second unbundling, and the first one explains why the second is possible. In 2024 Google signed a non-exclusive licensing agreement for Character.AI's large language model technology and rehired co-founders Noam Shazeer and Daniel De Freitas. Shazeer left Google in June to join OpenAI.

So the model license went to Google and the founders went with it. The IP license is going to Disney, and the staff already went there. What stays in the estate is the platform and its users, and the filing names the user base as the asset that matters. In its 4-year lifespan as an active platform, Character has amassed a large and engaged user base.

Engagement is also where the liabilities live. The filing blames high costs, "coupled with potential litigation related liabilities and regulatory engagement", for the debtor's inability to generate sufficient cash flows to support its business in the present form. In January the state of Kentucky sued the company, alleging the platform "preyed on children and led them into self-harm". Those allegations attach to the product people used, not to the weights Google licensed.

The FAQ is the interesting document

Five questions, five answers, no promises about outcomes. The platform remains up and running. Subscriptions remain active and billing is unchanged for now. Data privacy and security practices are unchanged. Users can export their data or delete their account.

Unchanged is not the same as protected. The company is describing what is true today and pointing at the export tool, which is reasonable, and also the thing you do when you cannot promise what happens next.

What the auction decides

The outcome turns on whether anyone bids for the platform as a going concern. The Disney offer is a floor on a license, and the transition services in that agreement exist to keep the platform safely running for a limited period while the auction plays out. If a higher or better offer arrives for the whole business, users and liabilities included, the platform continues under new ownership. If none does, $20 million buys a license to technology whose founders and staff have already left, and the users still logging in get wound down on a Delaware schedule.

Two dates matter more than the announcement: the court's approval of the bidding procedures, and the auction itself. Until the second one closes, "Character.AI filed for bankruptcy" and "Character.AI is shutting down" are different sentences.
